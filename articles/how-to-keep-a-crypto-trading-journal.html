How to Keep a Crypto Trading Journal That Works

A journal turns every trade, win or loss, into information you can learn from.

By Dilshad · 4 November 2026 · 4 min read

Process

A journal turns experience into learning

Dilshad is an independent cryptocurrency trader based in Trivandrum, Kerala, India. Over more than five years in digital-asset markets, one habit has stood out as the difference between repeating the same mistakes and slowly improving: writing every trade down. Memory is a poor record. It remembers the big wins, softens the painful losses and quietly rewrites the reasons behind decisions. A trading journal fixes that by keeping an honest record you can check later.

Why a journal works

A trade on its own proves very little, because a good decision can lose and a poor decision can win. A journal lets you judge the process instead of the result. After a few dozen entries, patterns appear that are impossible to see from memory: the hour of the day when you rush, the type of setup that keeps failing, the mood that leads to oversized positions. This fits the idea behind discipline versus luck, where the goal is a repeatable process and not a lucky outcome.

What to write before the trade

  • The reason. One or two sentences on why this trade exists. If you cannot explain it simply, it is not ready.
  • The exit that proves you wrong. The level or event at which the idea is no longer valid.
  • The size. How much is at stake and what share of your capital that is. See position sizing for how size follows from risk.
  • How you feel. A single word is enough, such as calm, excited, anxious or bored.

What to write after the trade

  • The result, in money and as a share of capital.
  • Did you follow the plan? A simple yes or no. A winning trade that broke the plan is a warning, not a success.
  • One lesson. What you would do the same way and what you would change.

The weekly review

The entries only pay off when you read them. Once a week, set aside a short time to go through the notes without judging yourself. Look for repeated words: "FOMO", "revenge", "hoped", "moved my exit". Count how many trades followed the plan and compare their results with the trades that did not. Over time this review shows where the real losses come from, and it is rarely where you expect.

Keep it simple

The best journal is the one you actually keep. A notebook, a spreadsheet or a notes app all work. Long forms are abandoned within a week, while five quick lines per trade survive. Record the losing trades as carefully as the winning ones, and never edit an entry afterwards to make it look better.

Common mistakes

  • Writing only after wins, which hides exactly the trades you need to study.
  • Changing the reason after the fact so the decision looks smarter.
  • Recording prices but not the thinking and feelings behind them.
  • Collecting entries and never reviewing them.

A record you can trust

A journal also supports risk management, because it shows whether you really keep to your own limits. It becomes more useful the longer you keep it, since a long record is something no memory can replace.
