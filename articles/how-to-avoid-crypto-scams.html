How to Avoid Crypto Scams: Warning Signs to Know

Most scams rely on the same few tricks. Knowing them is the best protection.

By Dilshad · 31 October 2026 · 4 min read

Safety

Most crypto scams use the same few tricks

Dilshad is an independent cryptocurrency trader based in Trivandrum, Kerala, India. Over more than five years of watching digital-asset markets, he has seen that losing money to a scam is far more common than losing it to a bad trade, and that the scams themselves are surprisingly repetitive. The names change and the platforms change, but the tricks stay the same. Learning to spot them is one of the cheapest forms of risk management a trader can practise.

Promises that sound too good

The most reliable warning sign is a promise of fixed, guaranteed or unusually high returns: "double your money in a month", "10 percent a week, no risk". Real markets move up and down, and nobody can guarantee a profit. Anyone who does is either mistaken or selling something. A useful rule is to treat the word "guaranteed" next to the word "crypto" as a reason to stop, not a reason to look closer.

Pressure and urgency

Scammers want a decision before you have time to check anything. Typical lines include "only a few spots left", "the offer ends tonight" and "invest now before the price jumps". A genuine opportunity survives a day of research. If a deal cannot wait while you verify it, it is not a deal you need.

Strangers who offer to trade for you

  • "Signal" and "tip" groups on messaging apps that promise inside knowledge, often run by people you cannot identify.
  • Trading managers who ask you to send money so they can "grow" it for a share of the profit.
  • Friendly strangers who build a relationship over weeks and then introduce an "amazing platform". Sending money to someone you have never met in person is the heart of this scam.

Fake platforms and fake dashboards

Some scam sites show a dashboard where your balance appears to grow. The numbers are invented. Withdrawals then fail, and the site asks for a "tax" or "unlock fee" before releasing your money. Paying it only loses more. Before putting money anywhere, check how long the platform has existed, whether independent sources describe it, and whether it is properly registered. In India, exchanges that serve Indian users are expected to register with the Financial Intelligence Unit (FIU-IND). Look up the registration yourself instead of trusting a badge on the website.

Impersonation and phishing

  • Fake support accounts that reply to your public questions and move you to a private chat.
  • Links in messages or emails that open a look-alike login page. Type the address yourself or use a saved bookmark.
  • Anyone asking for your seed phrase or private key. No real support team ever needs it. Whoever has it controls your wallet.

A short checklist before you send money

  • Is anything promised that no honest person could promise?
  • Am I being rushed?
  • Do I know exactly who is on the other side, and can I verify them independently?
  • Can I withdraw a small test amount first, and does it actually arrive?
  • Would I still do this if nobody else were doing it?

If something goes wrong

Stop sending money at once, since scammers often invent new fees to get more. Keep screenshots, wallet addresses, payment receipts and chat history. In India, report cyber fraud quickly through the National Cyber Crime Reporting Portal at cybercrime.gov.in or by calling the helpline 1930, because early reports give the best chance of freezing funds. Tell your bank as well.

Verify first, act second

Dilshad's approach to risk management starts before any trade: protect capital first and never put in more than you can afford to lose. Avoiding scams is part of the same habit. Slow down, check everything yourself, and remember that consistent results come from discipline, not from a tip that arrived in your inbox.
