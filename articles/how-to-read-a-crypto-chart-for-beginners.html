How to Read a Crypto Chart for Beginners

Candlesticks, timeframes, support and resistance, volume and why indicators are summaries, not signals.

By Dilshad · 4 November 2026 · 5 min read

Charts

A chart is a record of what already happened

Dilshad is an independent cryptocurrency trader based in Trivandrum, Kerala, India. Most beginners meet a price chart before they understand it, and the colours and lines can look like a promise about the future. They are not. A chart is a record of what buyers and sellers have already done. Reading it well means understanding that record, not predicting the next candle.

What a candlestick tells you

Each candlestick covers one period of time, such as one hour or one day. It shows four prices for that period: where it opened, where it closed, the highest point and the lowest point. The thick body spans the open and close. The thin lines above and below, called wicks, show how far the price travelled before it settled.

  • Colour. Most charts colour a candle one way when the price closed higher than it opened and the other way when it closed lower. Check your platform, because the colours are a setting, not a rule.
  • Body size. A large body means the price moved a long way in one direction during that period.
  • Wicks. A long wick means the price went somewhere and was pushed back. It shows disagreement between buyers and sellers.

Timeframes change the story

The same asset can look like it is rising on a one-hour chart and falling on a daily chart. Neither is wrong. They describe different spans of time. A useful habit is to look at a longer timeframe first to see the wider direction, and then a shorter one to see the detail. Your choice of timeframe should match how long you plan to hold, which connects to the choice described in day trading vs long-term holding.

Support and resistance

Support is a price area where a falling market has paused or turned before. Resistance is an area where a rising market has paused or turned before. Traders mark these areas because many people remember them and act near them. They are zones, not exact lines, and they do not hold forever. When price moves through a level it has respected before, that is information too, and it is a reminder to have a plan for being wrong.

Volume matters

Volume shows how much was traded in a period. A price move on heavy volume involved many participants. A move on thin volume may not mean much and can reverse easily. Volume does not tell you the direction of the next move, but it helps you judge how much weight to give the last one.

Indicators are summaries, not signals

Moving averages, the relative strength index and similar tools are calculations made from past prices. They summarise history in a different form. They can help you see the trend or how stretched a move has been, but they lag behind the price and they often disagree with each other. Adding more indicators does not add certainty. Many beginners cover a chart in lines and then cannot see the price. Start with price, volume and one or two tools you understand well.

Mistakes beginners make with charts

  • Treating a pattern as a guarantee. Patterns describe what has often happened, not what must happen.
  • Looking only at one timeframe and ignoring the bigger picture.
  • Changing indicators until the chart agrees with a trade they already want to take.
  • Trading without deciding in advance where the idea is proved wrong. See risk management and position sizing.

Use the chart as one part of a process

A chart shows price behaviour, but it says nothing about why people are buying or selling. That is why research before action matters alongside any chart work. Record what you saw and what you did in a trading journal, so you can check later whether your reading of charts is actually helping you.
